Mobility analysis · Allegiant Stadium district · Clark County, NV

The Walk In

How fans get from event parking to the stadium, and what role every street in the district should play.

Source: Azira Pinnacle pathing & visitation reports · anonymized, device-level GPS

What this tool is

A planning aid for the stadium district. It uses anonymized phone GPS pings to answer two questions, one after the other:

1. Where do people actually walk? The first maps trace the routes fans take on foot from event parking to the stadium: which streets carry the most people, who uses them, and how long the walk takes.

2. What should each street be built for? The last map gives every public street in the district one of three roles, so street design can match how the street is really used:

  • Gateways are arterials. They move people and vehicles into and out of the district.
  • Pedestrian streets are non-arterials that carry heavy walking. Candidates for wider sidewalks and amenities.
  • Local streets are everything else. Minimal treatment.

06 · From routes to street roles

Now give every street a job

The map switches to a single view of the whole district. The logic is deliberately simple:

  • Arterial becomes a gateway.
  • Heavy walking becomes a pedestrian street.
  • Everything else becomes a local street.

Roles come from all devices near any district building on all days, not only stadium trips. Event-day crowds would otherwise make every street look like a footpath.

08 · Pedestrian streets

Streets where people on foot dominate

A non-arterial becomes a pedestrian street only if it passes both tests:

  • it carries at least 5% of all walking lot-to-stadium trips, which is what the first maps show, and
  • it has at least 300 walking or standing observations on ordinary days in the district-wide data.

Today that is –. A street can carry heavy car traffic and still qualify. What counts is how many people walk it. These are the streets to design with wider sidewalks and pedestrian amenities.

09 · Local streets

Everything else needs only minimal treatment

– streets are access streets. Thin-data streets are drawn dashed to signal low confidence. Parking aisles, driveways and footways are left out.

10 · Event-day surges

Game days are flagged, never decisive

A yellow halo marks a street that is at least 10 points more pedestrian on event days than on ordinary days from the same months. Matching months matters because GPS ping frequency changes a lot through the year.

The halo is information for event operations. It never changes a street's role.

Method

Two analyses, two different questions

This page uses two separate sets of devices. They are kept apart on purpose, then brought together to support the street roles.

Volume · Local vs. Visitor · Approach Routes

The walk in from parking

Question: which streets do people use to get from a parking lot to the stadium, and how long does it take?

Population: only devices seen in a mapped parking lot and then at the stadium. It mostly reflects event days and walkers. These are the preferred walking routes that back every pedestrian-street designation.

Street Roles

The role each street plays

Question: which streets should be gateways, which pedestrian streets, and which local streets?

Population: every device seen near any building in the district over the whole data period, including ordinary non-event days, so vehicle traffic is not drowned out by event crowds.

The three street roles
  • Gateway: an arterial (OpenStreetMap motorway, trunk, primary or secondary). Designed to move people and vehicles into and out of the district.
  • Pedestrian street: a non-arterial that carries at least 5% of all walking lot-to-stadium trips and has at least 300 walking or standing observations on ordinary days. Designed with wider sidewalks and amenities.
  • Local street: everything else. Minimal treatment. Thin-data streets are drawn dashed.
  • An arterial that is also a major walk-in route keeps the gateway role and is noted in its tooltip as a dual-role street.
  • Parking-lot aisles, driveways and footways are left out. Only public streets are typed.
Why two populations? The skew we found

Typing streets only from stadium-anchored trips labeled almost every street a pedestrian corridor, even Dean Martin Drive and Tropicana Avenue. Event dates were about 42% of device-days in that extract. A broader district-wide extract cut event dates to about 10% of device-days, and the major arterials then read as vehicle roads.

How event days are handled

The district-wide data supplies each street's ordinary-day pedestrian volume, and event days cannot create a role on their own. Event-day behavior is reported separately as the yellow surge halo: a rise of at least 10 points in pedestrian share compared with ordinary days from the same months. GPS ping frequency varies a lot by month (median gap about 3 seconds in early 2025, about 40 seconds in summer 2025), which would otherwise look like an event effect.

Limits to keep in mind
  • Travel mode is inferred from speed between GPS pings, which is imprecise when pings are sparse. Slow or stop-and-go cars near the Strip can look like bikes, so for street typing "biking" counts as vehicle traffic.
  • Pedestrian observations are relative signals from speed, not counted pedestrians. That is why pedestrian streets must also be supported by measured lot-to-stadium walking trips.
  • Walk-in routes come from stadium trips, so a street away from the stadium path will not qualify as a pedestrian street here even if people walk it.
  • The thresholds (5% of walking trips, 300 observations) are judgment calls and are adjustable in the code.
  • Census commute-mode data for the district's block group (about 623 workers) was reviewed as a plausibility check only. It describes residents, not people who work in the district.

Full methodology, thresholds and code are in the repository README.

Methodology. Trips are reconstructed from an Azira Pinnacle Pathing X export, with stadium arrival independently confirmed by a Pin report and spatially matched against the exact stadium footprint. A device only appears here if it pinged inside a mapped lot polygon and reached the stadium within the study's post-window. Residency is derived from each device's Common Evening Location relative to the Las Vegas metro area. Lots with fewer than 38 qualifying devices are flagged low-confidence.

Street segments are matched offline, in the Python pipeline, against OpenStreetMap street-centerline geometry (via --street-match), not guessed live in the browser. Segment color and thickness show device volume; the animated flow shows whether a segment carries more inbound or outbound traffic for the current filter.
