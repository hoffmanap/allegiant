Method — how to read this page

Two analyses, two different questions

This page answers two separate questions with two separate sets of devices. Mixing them up is the easiest way to misread the maps, so they are kept apart on purpose.

Maps 1–2 · Volume, Local vs. Visitor

The walk in from parking

Question: which streets do people use to get from a parking lot to the stadium, and how long does it take?

Population: only devices seen in a mapped parking lot and then at the stadium. Because it is anchored to stadium visits, it mostly reflects event days and pedestrians.

Maps 3–4 · Street Type

How the district's streets are used

Question: which streets are vehicle gateways, which are pedestrian corridors, and which are local access streets?

Population: every device seen near any building in the district over the whole data period (a broader extract that includes ordinary, non-event days), so vehicle traffic is not drowned out by event-day crowds.

Why two populations? (the skew we found)

The first street typing used only stadium-anchored trips and labeled almost every street a pedestrian corridor, even major roads like Dean Martin Drive and Tropicana Avenue. We checked the data: event dates were about 42% of device-days in that extract, with roughly 5× the activity of other days. A broader district-wide extract cut event dates to about 10% of device-days, and the major arterials then came out as gateways.

How streets are typed
  • Gateway — a major road (OpenStreetMap primary / secondary / tertiary) where vehicle travel dominates.
  • Pedestrian corridor — a non-gateway street where, on ordinary (non-event) days, at least half of classified observations look like walking or standing, with enough data to trust it.
  • Event-day pedestrian corridor — pedestrian share jumps at least 10 points on event days compared with ordinary days from the same months, and reaches at least 50%.
  • Local street — everything else with enough data. Streets with too little routine data are shown grey as insufficient data.
  • Parking-lot aisles, driveways and footways are left out; only real public streets are typed.
How event days are handled (without letting them skew the result)

Each street's label comes from non-event days only, so a crowded game day cannot turn a road into a "pedestrian street." Event days are reported separately as a flag. The event comparison uses ordinary days from the same months, because GPS ping frequency varies a lot by month (median gap of about 3 seconds in early 2025 versus about 40 seconds in summer 2025), which would otherwise look like an event effect.

Limits to keep in mind
  • Travel mode is inferred from speed between GPS pings, which is imprecise when pings are sparse. Slow or stop-and-go cars near the Strip can look like bikes; for street typing we count "biking" as vehicle traffic.
  • The shares are relative rankings, not measured pedestrian counts. Even the busiest arterials read only about 60–65% vehicle.
  • Streets with few observations are marked insufficient data or low confidence rather than forced into a category.
  • Census commute-mode data for the district's block group (about 623 workers) was reviewed as a plausibility check only; it describes residents, not people who work in the district.

Full methodology, thresholds and code are in the repository README.

Methodology. Trips are reconstructed from an Azira Pinnacle Pathing X export, with stadium arrival independently confirmed by a Pin report and spatially matched against the exact stadium footprint. A device only appears here if it pinged inside a mapped lot polygon and reached the stadium within the study's post-window; devices that never showed a confirmed arrival in that window were excluded as noise. Residency is derived from each device's Common Evening Location relative to the Las Vegas metro area. Lots with fewer than 38 qualifying devices are flagged low-confidence.

Street segments are matched offline, in the Python pipeline, against real OpenStreetMap street-centerline geometry (via --street-match) — not guessed live in the browser. Segment color and thickness show device volume; the animated flow direction shows whether a segment carries more inbound or outbound traffic for the current filter. "Devices per segment" and "entry points" both recompute live as you change the lot/residency filters, since the underlying data shipped to the browser is each trip's matched street sequence, not a single pre-baked total.
